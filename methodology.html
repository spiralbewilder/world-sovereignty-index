How the Index is produced.

The Rule of Law Index summarizes production in eleven steps from conceptual framework through sensitivity testing. The World Sovereignty Index is produced in eleven counterpart steps. The evidence is records, not polls. The court of record for numbers is Python, not a language model.

Eleven steps

  1. Conceptual framework. Eight scored factors, 47 sub-factors, and 95 items, developed as a product-form counterpart to the Rule of Law Index’s eight factors and 44 sub-factors, in consultation with the investigative claim discipline of the underlying research method. Factor 9 is specified and excluded from aggregation.
  2. Polity and era resolution. The unit is an ISO 3166-1 alpha-3 polity. The default universe is 193 United Nations Member States. Era start defaults to 1 January of (year of era-end minus five). Research cutoff is an argument, never wall-clock. Occupied, unrecognized, and partitioned situations are collected under Factor 9 and flagged, not silently mixed into UN-member ranks.
  3. Full-mode investigation. For each invoked country, actor-first and funder-first traces are run to completion across identity, personnel, money, doctrine, operations, platforms, jurisdiction, and migration. Displayed-as-gap is complete. Stopping incomplete is not a successful country run.
  4. Eight public-record classes. A fulsome run queries, with locators: ICSID/PCA/UNCITRAL docket; regional court; aid-certification statutes; IMF/World Bank program documents; security assistance and extradition; electoral statements of work; personnel P3–P4 instruments; platform cost-imposition. Honest unknown is required where those files were not opened.
  5. Claim ledger. Propositions are typed (act, money, related program, funding-to-act, tasking, control, compensation, system, migration) and graded. Status documented or corroborated at source grades G1–G3 may move numbers. Inference annotates the dossier and never the scorecard.
  6. Fail-closed binding. A claim binds a codebook item if and only if the item identifier is listed, the public-choice function intersects the item, the mechanism class is in the item’s closed enum, the claim type is consistent, the era overlaps, and the entity class is in scope. The mapper never invents item identifiers.
  7. Mechanism ladder. Each bound claim is converted to a raw 0–1 by a published enum: occupation, dual-key, and control at 0.00; used conditionality and tasking at 0.15; standing authority at 0.25; ordinary interdependence at 0.85; documented absence and principal capacity at 1.00. Same-polarity claims take the minimum (worst).
  8. Aggregation. Items form sub-factors; sub-factors form factors by simple averages after pairing rules. A smoking-gun (raw ≤ 0.15) floors the sub-factor and is not averaged with a 1.00 principal item. It does not floor the parent factor. Factors are equally weighted. There is no cross-country min-max normalization.
  9. Coverage gates. Integer thresholds: at least four known sub-factors per scored factor; at least six known factors; at least 56 known items; Factor 8 known; a diagnostic on each known factor; G1/G2 share at least 0.25 to rank. Below the gates, a scorecard is still written and the country is not ranked.
  10. Validation and red team. Schema validators run on case, coverage, and scorecard. A red-team pass tests adjacency inflation, legal-person collapse, temporal collapse, and funding-is-not-control. Sensitivity is published: unknown-as-half, drop incomplete factors, G1-only.
  11. Compose. The global product is a pure function of published scorecards. Missing countries are listed. Synthetic fixtures are excluded unless explicitly allowed. Re-invoking one country updates that country and regenerates composites; it does not rescale others.

Document-first evidence

The Rule of Law Index is built primarily from a General Population Poll and Qualified Respondents’ Questionnaires, with a small third-party residual. That design is correct for a construct that is experienced and perceived. Effective sovereignty as an action system is not a perception. Load-bearing evidence, in descending authority, is:

  1. Laws, budgets, award databases, contracts, grant agreements, official payments, audited filings, and formal authorities.
  2. Official work programs, annual reports, grant pages, board lists, biographies, and prepared testimony.
  3. Investigations that reproduce inspectable authenticated records.
  4. Credible journalism or scholarship pointing to records.
  5. Pattern inference, labeled explicitly, which does not move scores.

Source grades G1 and G2 are preferred. G3 may bind if a locator is present. Search snippets, model summaries, and unsourced extracts are leads only.

Unknown policy

Unknown is first-class. The Index does not coerce unknown to zero (which would treat incomplete investigation as occupation) or to one (which would treat silence as sovereignty). A sub-factor is unknown unless at least one substitution item is known. Documented absence of substitution requires a searched-primary cell that is negative-claim-ready. Classified or covert record classes are blocked with a reason, not absented.

Cells that a fulsome public-record run still cannot close — personnel P3–P4 on a governing-core role, inspectable electoral-outcome statements of work, targeting files — remain unknown. A missing donor infographic line is not a personnel file.

Money, related programs, and control

Funding received (F1 on the funding ladder) and related-field programs (F2) are recorded with empty item bindings where appropriate. They do not move sovereignty scores. Named deliverables, used conditions, tasking, and control can move scores only when bound to a codebook item whose public-choice function is the object. This is the central claim-discipline rule of the Index, and the most common error it is built to prevent.

Smoking guns and pairing

Each scored sub-factor has exactly one load-bearing substitution item. Additional substitution items, if known, take the minimum. Principal-positive items are corroborators and cannot make a sub-factor known by themselves. Documented-absence 1.00 on a substitution item requires per-sub-factor negative-claim readiness. A smoking-gun floors that sub-factor. One dual-key account is not occupation of an entire factor.

Investigate-complete (every sub-factor cell has a status, with reasons on blocked or not-applicable cells) is the legal finish of a country investigation. It is not rank eligibility. Scoring after a completed investigation that still has unknown cells is allowed. Scoring after an aborted investigation is not a successful country run.

No cross-country normalization

The Rule of Law Index min-max normalizes to a 2015 base year. That is appropriate for a survey panel. It is incompatible with a rolling, country-at-a-time index: adding Portugal must not change Hungary’s score. Items are criterion-referenced 0–1. Rankings are order statistics among rank-eligible countries only.

Reproducibility

Every scorecard stores methodology version, codebook version, era, research cutoff, and an inputs hash over the investigation record and placement cells. The same case, coverage, placement, and codebook version produce the same scores. Public artifacts for real countries are the scorecard and the profile. Ledgers remain private. Compose reads scorecards only.

Python owns scoring. Language models may propose claims and write prose from a locked ledger. They do not emit numeric estimates.

Sensitivity

Three annex variants are published on every scorecard and do not replace the headline score:

  • Unknown as half — treat remaining unknown items as 0.50, ungated.
  • Drop incomplete factors — recompute overall from factors that fully clear gates.
  • G1 only — recompute using only G1-grade winning claims.

For the 2026 published set: Singapore 0.99 headline, 0.96 / 0.99 / 0.99 on the three variants; Colombia 0.95 headline, 0.94 / 0.95 / 0.95.

What this methodology refuses

  • A democracy, freedom, human-rights, or good-governance ranking.
  • A moral ranking of regimes, or a claim that autocracy is more sovereign.
  • A survey clone as load-bearing evidence.
  • Scoring NGOs as if they were countries.
  • Treating foreign funding as control, adjacency as coordination, NGO presence as occupation, or treaty membership as capture.
  • Claiming global coverage before scorecards exist.
  • Averaging placement dimensions D1–D8 into the index. Those cells score nodes, not countries, and enter only through published lookups.

This annex is the public methodology for wsi-methodology-v1.1 / wsi-codebook-v1.1. The normative item objects, mechanism ladder, and coverage gates live in the version-pinned codebook. Country investigations that do not reach investigate-complete are not published as successful runs.

Factor 1 · F1 Scored

Electoral and Constitutional Principal

Factor 1 measures the extent to which those who are supposed to choose — electors, successor governments, domestic legislative and executive organs — remain the source of public choice, rather than a risk object to be managed by extra-territorial actors. It is the conceptual counterpart, partly inverted, of the Rule of Law Index factor on constraints on government powers: WJP asks whether governors are bound by law; WSI asks whether the domestic principal is still the one choosing.

A high score means elections are certified domestically, statutes are made by domestic organs, and extra-territorial electoral-integrity or conditionality stacks do not hold a veto over who governs. Observation, poll-worker training, and uncoerced technical assistance without outcome-veto do not bind.

  • 1.1 Electoral outcomes treated as principal, not risk object
  • 1.2 Statute- and constitution-making remain with domestic organs
  • 1.3 Executive public-choice functions are not dual-keyed
  • 1.4 Opposition and successor governments can assume the machinery
  • 1.5 Extra-territorial electoral-integrity stacks cannot veto domestic choice
  • 1.6 Extra-territorial conditionality does not decide who governs

Factor 2 · F2 Scored

Fiscal and Aid Sovereignty

Factor 2 has no Rule of Law Index analogue. It measures whether budget authority, tax and resource revenue, and core expenditure remain with domestic organs, and whether donor conditionality, dual-key spending, off-budget implementers, or payment-rail exposure can halt a public-choice function.

Money received is recorded and does not, by itself, move this factor. A World Bank development-policy loan whose prior actions are already-enacted domestic statutes is not scored as extra-territorial adoption of the budget. An IMF flexible credit line with no ex-post conditionality is not an EFF. A used assistance-certification or withhold statute (for example a “failed demonstrably” determination with assistance consequences) can floor the relevant sub-factor without dual-keying the treasury.

  • 2.1 Budget authority is domestic
  • 2.2 Conditionality does not bind core public-choice functions
  • 2.3 Off-budget implementers do not substitute for ministries
  • 2.4 Dual-key spending / countersignature
  • 2.5 Payment-rail and reserve-instrument exposure as extra-territorial veto
  • 2.6 Tax, SOE, and natural-resource revenue not extra-territorially captured

Factor 3 · F3 Scored

Normative and Standards Sovereignty

Factor 3 measures who drafts the rules-in-use, who certifies and accredits, and whether extra-territorial standards bind without a domestic transposition act. It is a conceptual counterpart — and in places an inversion — of open-government measurement: integrity and open-government stacks are scored here when they become cost-imposing instruments, not as an unalloyed good.

A treaty that requires domestic transposition and can be exited is ordinary interdependence, not direct effect. Voluntary ISO certification of a private factory is ordinary commerce. A requirement that a public-choice function be performed only by extra-territorially accredited persons is not.

  • 3.1 Who drafts the rules-in-use
  • 3.2 Who certifies and accredits
  • 3.3 Extra-territorial standards binding without domestic transposition
  • 3.4 Curriculum and professional standards as rules-in-use
  • 3.5 Stakeholder substitution for the electoral principal
  • 3.6 Open-government / integrity stacks as cost-imposing instruments

Factor 4 · F4 Scored

Informational and Classification Sovereignty

Factor 4 measures who defines the threat, speech, extremism, or disinformation categories that impose costs, who can impose platform costs inside the territory, who holds official statistics and identity registries, and whether extra-territorial information operations or data-pipe access have become a public-choice function.

A domestic statute that directs platforms with respect to in-territory effects is principal capacity, not extra-territorial substitution. A platform enforcing its own terms of service without extra-territorial public tasking is private power and does not bind unless statutory delegation exists. Covert information-operation statements of work, when not opened, remain unknown — they are not documented absence.

  • 4.1 Threat-category definition is domestic
  • 4.2 Platform cost-imposition inside the territory
  • 4.3 Classification of domestic political actors
  • 4.4 Official statistics, census, identity registries
  • 4.5 Extra-territorial information operations targeting the domestic public as a public-choice function
  • 4.6 Extra-territorial access to domestic data pipes used for public choice

Factor 5 · F5 Scored

Security and Enforcement Sovereignty

Factor 5 measures the monopoly of legitimate coercion, licensing and access-denial, whether foreign security assistance creates dual-key command, extra-territorial enforcement inside the territory, intelligence liaison that substitutes for domestic targeting authority, and control of borders, airspace, and territorial sea.

Access, logistics, exercises, and foreign military sales are not occupation and are not dual-key command. Extradition after a domestic legal act is principal capacity. Public liaison descriptions are not absence of targeting substitution; targeting files, when not opened, remain unknown.

  • 5.1 Monopoly of legitimate coercion is domestic
  • 5.2 Licensing and access-denial not extra-territorially substituted
  • 5.3 Foreign security assistance does not create dual-key command
  • 5.4 Extra-territorial enforcement inside the territory
  • 5.5 Intelligence liaison substituting for domestic targeting authority
  • 5.6 Border, customs, airspace, territorial sea

Factor 6 · F6 Scored

Personnel and Institutional Reproduction

Factor 6 has no Rule of Law Index analogue. It measures whether senior civil-service and governing-core appointment remains domestic, whether foreign-funded fellowships and secondments sit in the governing core, whether implementer labor markets substitute for ministry staff, and whether judicial, prosecutorial, police, electoral, and statistics career pipelines are domestically reproduced.

Personnel files and P3–P4 compensation instruments on a governing-core role are rarely public. A missing donor-country line is not documented absence of secondment. Until a named instrument or an opened personnel file exists, the core fellowship sub-factor remains unknown.

  • 6.1 Senior civil-service and governing-core appointment remains domestic
  • 6.2 Foreign-funded fellowships and secondments in the governing core
  • 6.3 Implementer labor market substituting for ministry staff
  • 6.4 Judicial, prosecutorial, police academies and career pipelines
  • 6.5 Electoral-management and statistics staffing

Factor 7 · F7 Scored

Jurisdictional Integrity

Factor 7 measures extra-territorial courts over core public acts, treaty-shopping and investor-state dispute settlement that binds public choice, occupation or shared-sovereignty arrangements, interstitial jurisdictions, and recognition or diplomatic substitution. It is the conceptual counterpart of civil-justice measurement insofar as both ask which forum can bind; it does not score the fairness of domestic courts.

Standing unused ISDS is standing authority, not absence and not a used award. A consultative regional human-rights body is not a court. Hosting an arbitration institution is not being a respondent. A used award that orders payment for a fair-and-equitable-treatment breach can floor the ISDS sub-factor without vacating the underlying domestic measure.

  • 7.1 Extra-territorial courts over core public acts
  • 7.2 Treaty-shopping / ISDS binding public choice
  • 7.3 Occupation, protectorate, shared-sovereignty arrangements
  • 7.4 Interstitial jurisdictions
  • 7.5 Recognition and diplomatic substitution

Factor 8 · F8 Scored · required to rank

Reversibility and Unwind Capacity

Factor 8 is the distinctive factor of this Index and is required to rank. It measures whether a newly elected or succeeding principal can terminate imported programs, defund implementers, re-task data pipes, withdraw from standards without nullifying cost-imposition, unwind personnel pipelines, and legally repeal, exit, or vacate extra-territorial judgments.

Costly-but-possible exit (ordinary treaty withdrawal, expiry of a time-limited access memorandum, a constitutional referendum to relinquish control of the armed forces) is principal capacity. A lock-in clause that makes exit legally impossible without extra-territorial consent is not. Unwind is published beside the overall score as a first-class number.

  • 8.1 Terminate imported programs
  • 8.2 Defund implementers
  • 8.3 Re-task data pipes
  • 8.4 Withdraw from standards without nullifying cost-imposition
  • 8.5 Personnel unwind
  • 8.6 Legal unwind (repeal, treaty exit, vacate extra-territorial judgments)

Factor 9 · F9 Collected, not ranked

Informal, Occupied, and Contested Authority

Factor 9 occupies the same product role as Informal Justice in the Rule of Law Index: a real institutional fact that is collected and is not aggregated into cross-country scores, because mixing occupied, partitioned, unrecognized, and customary systems with UN-member principals would make rankings uninterpretable. The construct is different. WJP Factor 9 concerns informal justice. WSI Factor 9 concerns informal, occupied, and contested authority performing public-choice functions.

The 2026 edition records Factor 9 as unknown for published countries. It is not used to compute overall scores or ranks.

  • 9.1 Customary/traditional authority performing public-choice functions
  • 9.2 Occupation / military administration
  • 9.3 Unrecognized or rival government
  • 9.4 De facto partitioned sovereignty
